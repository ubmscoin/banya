Resolution of the theta Unit (mined 2026-08-31)

Why a dimensionless ratio reads as radians: on the unit circle, arc length and angle are the same number

Theorem D. The unit-circle form of the Banya equation (Axiom 1 proposition) is $\delta = e^{i\theta}$ with radius 1. On a circle of radius 1 the arc length $s$ and the central angle $\theta$ satisfy $s = \theta$: the length scale and the angle scale are not separable there.

The Axiom 4 proposition (reading cost as a norm) reads one and the same $+$ crossing in two units, cost reading (integer) and norm reading (arc length). The place where the two readings meet is the unit circle, and there arc length equals angle, so a dimensionless cost-reading ratio is already a phase angle. $\theta = 2/9$ is the Compare degrees of freedom 2 over the complete descriptive degrees of freedom 9 (H-22), and on the unit circle that ratio is the phase in radians. No conversion constant can enter.

Numerical discrimination. The optimal phase demanded by measurement is $0.2222258$ rad, differing from $2/9 = 0.2222222$ by $1.6\times10^{-5}$ relative. Candidates multiplied by $\pi$ ($2\pi/9$, $\pi/9$, $4\pi/9$) blow the reconstruction up by hundreds to thousands of percent. Only reading the dimensionless ratio directly as radians closes against measurement, and Theorem D supplies the axiomatic ground.

Why not a $\pi$ conversion. The $n\pi$ of norm reading is the arc length of one irreversible $+$ crossing (Axiom 4 proposition). $\theta$ is not a crossing count but an occupancy ratio of degrees of freedom, hence not a crossing-accounted quantity. In the two-reading split $\theta$ belongs to cost reading, and the unit circle carries that value over as an angle.
