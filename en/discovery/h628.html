Ferro and antiferro share the same lock-bit alignment (H-627); they are two branches that differ only in the sign of the exchange cost $J$ binding neighbors. The sign of $J$ is split by the ECS neighbor distance (Axiom 7), and the order locks in the cost-minimizing direction (Axiom 4).

Step 2. Norm Substitution

The norm being substituted is the coupling cost of a single neighboring lock-bit pair, namely the Heisenberg coupling norm. The dot product of a bit pair, not a single bit, is substituted because what separates ferro from antiferro is not the bit itself but the coupling sign between two bits. The sign of the coupling norm fixes the alignment direction.

$H = -J\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}\mathbf{S}_i\cdot\mathbf{S}_j$
$J > 0:\ \uparrow\uparrow\ (\text{ferro}), \quad J < 0:\ \uparrow\downarrow\ (\text{antiferro})$
$H$: coupling cost | $J$: ECS neighbor exchange cost (Axiom 7) | $\mathbf{S}_i$: entity lock bit | $J>0$: parallel minimum cost | $J<0$: antiparallel minimum cost

Step 3. Constant Substitution

J > 0 : Fe, Co, Ni ferro (parallel)
J < 0 : Cr, MnO antiferro (antiparallel)
$T_{N}(Cr)$ = 311 K Neel temperature
H = -J $S_{i}$ . $S_{j}$ Heisenberg model

Step 4. Domain Transformation

The transformation is into the magnetic-order domain. What it reveals is the ground-state alignment forced by the coupling sign. When $J$ is positive, parallel alignment is the cost minimum; when negative, alternating antiparallel alignment is the cost minimum.

$J > 0$ gives parallel, $\;J < 0$ gives alternating antiparallel
