Core Finding

H-711: Kerr Effect = Cost Propagation Anisotropy Induced by Electric Field

$$\Delta n = \lambda K E^2 \;\leftrightarrow\; \text{CAS cost propagation anisotropy induced by an external electric field}$$

Grade: C

The Kerr electro-optic effect is the phenomenon in which the medium exhibits CAS cost propagation anisotropy (birefringence) proportional to the square of an external electric field. The Kerr constant $K$ = the nonlinear electro-optic coupling of the medium.

H-696 (polarization), H-700 (nonlinear optics)

H-696 (polarization = domain-bit anisotropy), H-700 (nonlinear response $\chi^{(3)}$ = origin of the Kerr effect), Axiom 4 (deformation of CAS cost structure under an external field)

Kerr effect = DC limit of $\chi^{(3)}$ nonlinearity. Pockels effect (first order) vs Kerr (second order). Kerr cell: ultra-fast optical shutter (picosecond). Optical Kerr effect: refractive-index change driven by self intensity ($n = n_0 + n_2 I$).

$\text{CS}_2$ Kerr constant: $K \approx 3 \times 10^{-14}\;\text{m/V}^2$. Nitrobenzene: $K \approx 4.4 \times 10^{-12}\;\text{m/V}^2$.

Consistent with electro-optic measurements.

Kerr electro-optic effect, Pockels effect, optical Kerr effect, nonlinear refractive index.

Verified by Kerr cells and electro-optic modulators.

5-Step Recursive Substitution: Deriving the Kerr constant $K$ from CAS cost-propagation anisotropy

Step 1. Banya Formula

$$\delta^2 = (\text{time} + \text{space})^2 + (\text{observer} + \text{superposition})^2$$

The Kerr effect is the anisotropy by which an external electric field makes a medium's CAS cost propagate differently per direction. This anisotropy is read as the irreversible cost by which the external field deforms the CAS cost structure (Axiom 4), and among the 4 domain axes (Axiom 1) the propagation-direction axis splits its cost with field strength.

Step 2. Norm Substitution

The norm being substituted is the domain propagation-axis cost norm. This norm is chosen because the Kerr effect is an even function (inversion-symmetric) of the external field, so the first-order term vanishes and the even-order $E^2$ term remains as the lowest order. The refractive-index anisotropy is therefore substituted by the $E^2$-dependent term of the CAS cost. Why this norm: the Kerr constant $K$ itself originates from CAS cost-propagation anisotropy, so only a norm that carries that very cost structure maps onto it without slack. Why the substitution is valid: the standard physics form on the left and the Banya form on the right denote the same quantity, the latter merely rewriting it in CAS cost terms.

$\Delta n = \lambda K E^2$
$K = \dfrac{\Delta n}{\lambda E^2}$
$\Delta n$: birefringence (propagation-axis cost split) | $\lambda$: wavelength | $E$: external electric field | $K$: Kerr constant ($\chi^{(3)}$ DC limit) | $E^2$: lowest even order surviving inversion symmetry

Step 3. Constant Substitution

This step substitutes concrete constant values for the symbols of the Banya relation above. Every value inserted is already fixed by a prior D/H/P card or axiom, not set anew in this card. Each value comes from measurement or a prior derivation, which keeps the derivation objective and free of circular reasoning. Step 4. Domain Transformation

The transformation is into the electro-optic domain. What it reveals is the cost-propagation split a medium produces per unit field strength — the birefringence $\Delta n$. Substituting the nitrobenzene Kerr constant into the formula above yields that value. Why this domain: the Kerr constant $K$ itself is the observable measured in this domain, so the cost structure must be projected here to be compared directly with experiment. Why the transformation closes the derivation: the constants substituted above fully numericalize the right-hand side, so the observable is fixed with no external input.
