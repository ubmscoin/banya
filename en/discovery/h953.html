$$D^{\alpha} \;\leftrightarrow\; e^{i\alpha\pi/2}, \qquad D^{1/2} \;\leftrightarrow\; e^{i\pi/4} = \frac{1+i}{\sqrt{2}}$$

Structural description — Fourier phase $\alpha\pi/2$ · Lacroix(1819) $D^{1/2}x = 2\sqrt{x/\pi}$ · semi-group $D^{1/2}\!\circ\!D^{1/2}=D$ agreement

Differentiation is an orthogonal rotation inside OPERATOR (the quantum bracket, continuous and reversible). DATA is discrete so differentiation is forbidden (Axiom 3); it is defined only in the continuous OPERATOR. First-order differentiation is a $\pi/2$ rotation onto the adjacent orthogonal axis, and order-$\alpha$ differentiation is expressed as $D^{\alpha} \leftrightarrow e^{i\alpha\pi/2}$ — reversible (integration is its inverse), not the irreversible half-sphere $\pi$ of Axiom 2.

Substituting $\alpha=1/2$ gives a $\pi/4$ rotation = $e^{i\pi/4}=(1+i)/\sqrt{2}$. This agrees exactly with Lacroix(1819) $D^{1/2}(x)=2\sqrt{x/\pi}$ and $D^{1/2}(\sin x)=\sin(x+\pi/4)$ — the half-derivative Leibniz raised to L'Hôpital in 1695 closes as half of the orthogonal-rotation phase.

Two half-derivatives = $\pi/4 + \pi/4 = \pi/2$ = one integer derivative. A structural reading of the identity $D^{1/2}\!\circ\!D^{1/2}=D$ (semi-group property), following automatically from the additivity of rotation angles.

A reversible rotation angle has no cost reading and is expressed only as an orthogonal-rotation phase. This is the structural reason fractional calculus has existed as a mathematical tool not directly captured by experiment.
